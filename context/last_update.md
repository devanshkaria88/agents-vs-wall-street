Good challenge — and the Primer guy's advice is *correct for the architecture most teams in that room are running*. Let me untangle where the variance comes from, because that determines whether averaging helps.

**Why they're averaging.** If your agent is end-to-end stochastic — "LLM, research this company and give me a number" — then the number itself comes out of the model's sampling process. Each run wanders a different evidence path, weighs things differently, and emits a different figure: 46.9B this run, 47.4B the next. That spread is real variance, and averaging N runs is the textbook fix — each run is a noisy draw, the mean converges, outlier runs wash out. For that shape of agent, multi-run averaging is the single best cheap upgrade, and he's right to tell people to do it.

**Why it buys us ~nothing at the final-number level.** Averaging reduces *variance*; it cannot reduce *bias*. Our final numbers don't come out of a sampling process — they come out of `45,277 × (1 + organic) + GMS` on firewall-verified quotes. Run it 100 times, get 100 identical answers; the average of a constant is the constant. In fact, the ensemble approach is a Monte Carlo approximation of what our engine computes in closed form: their N runs converge toward "what the model, anchored on the docs, thinks on average" — we just compute that quantity directly and show the working. Averaging our pipeline would be averaging away noise we already removed by design. Whether we *win* is decided entirely by whether the anchors and adjustments are right — bias, not variance — and no amount of re-running fixes a wrong anchor.
